# AstroAtlas — v3

### Know your own story first. Then you can see everyone else's.

---

## 1. Purpose

AstroAtlas exists to help you remember who you are beneath the masks you wore to be loved.

Astrology is the doorway, not the room. Here, it serves as a symbolic language — unusually rich and specific — for looking at yourself without flinching, and then at the people around you with the same tenderness.

**Founding Principle:** *Know yourself before you try to know anyone else. The rarest skill in human relationship is seeing each person in their true shape — their real strengths, their real limits — rather than the shortcut your mind creates in the first three seconds.*

*When you disarm your own stereotypes, you become both kinder and more realistic. And each time you do this for another, you understand your own nature a little more.*

> *Internal note: written as AstroAtlas's own conviction, not as a quotation. If a source is later identified, it will be credited with care.*

## 2. Who it's for

Astrology-curious people of any age — with a product centre of gravity in **Millennials and Gen Z**, and a deliberate role for **Gen X and Baby Boomers**.

The reason to include the older generations is not market size. It's that **the pattern you want to change usually predates you.** The conditioning a person is trying to see clearly was largely installed by people whose own conditioning came from a different world. You cannot read your own script without some sense of who wrote it.

### Generation Y / Millennials (≈1981–1996)
First true digital natives, shaped by the internet and globalisation, marked by 9/11 and the crises that followed. They seek meaning in what they do, value flexibility, team orientation and balance, and stayed broadly optimistic despite the shocks.

→ *They will engage with depth and length. Most likely first paying users. The relational layer is for them.*

### Generation Z (≈1996–2012)
Fully digital. Compared with Gen Y they want security, clear structures, and a firm boundary between work and private life. Globally connected, socially engaged, social media woven through daily life. Work is one part of a stable life, not the source of meaning itself.

→ *Two decisions follow. First: clear structure wins — the 21-day cycle with one focus per day is exactly their shape; open-ended "explore your chart" is not. Second: never frame this as a work tool. A generation that guards the work/life boundary will reject self-knowledge sold as workplace optimisation.*

### Generation X (≈1965–1980)
The "slacker" or "golf" generation: pragmatic, independent, insistent on separating work from private life. They lived the analogue-to-digital transition. Work was often a means to financial security and status rather than an identity.

→ *Pragmatists. They disengage fast from mysticism-heavy language and anything that calls itself a "journey." Plain speech, visible usefulness, no incense. They are also frequently the person being **added** — the parent whose chart explains something — rather than the primary user.*

### Baby Boomers (≈1946–1964)
Born into the post-war birth surge and a period of real prosperity. Suburbanisation, and the cultural movements — civil rights, counterculture — that reshaped the society they grew up in. Strong work ethic, loyalty to employers, material achievement. Parents of most Gen X and Millennials.

→ *Many came of age when astrology entered the mainstream, so openness is often higher than expected. Two practical consequences: interface accessibility can't assume app fluency, and **birth times are far more often unknown or unrecorded for this generation** — which makes the no-time mode (§6) load-bearing rather than a nice-to-have.*

## 3. The core: the 21-day cycle

One cycle, three weeks, one focus per day.

Each day pairs **one thing from the chart to notice** with **one universal practice to work with it** (§4). The chart gives the direction of attention; the practice does the actual work.

### Week 1 — Your Gift (days 1–7)
What you are naturally built to do. Recognition, not flattery. "You're creative" is useless. "You solve problems by circling them sideways, which is why direct instruction irritates you" is usable.

### Week 2 — The Cost of the Gift (days 8–14)
The same strengths from their shadow side.

**Deliberately not called "your weaknesses."** Every limitation is shown as the price of a specific gift — the overextension, the blind spot, the thing that protects you and also costs you. Loyalty becomes difficulty leaving. Precision becomes paralysis. Warmth becomes an inability to say no.

More accurate psychologically, and far safer for an audience already saturated in self-criticism. **Structural rule: no limitation ever appears without the strength it belongs to.**

### Week 3 — The Others (days 15–21)
One or two more charts, added with consent. Each day: one quality of that person you had flattened into a label, looked at properly. What are they protecting? What are they good at that you've discounted because it isn't your way?

Ends in something actionable — a question to ask, a thing to say, an assumption to drop.

#### Week 3 when you're on your own

Most people won't have a second person ready — that's the biggest drop-off point in the whole product. Two things fill it, and neither requires anyone else's data:

**The Inception Chart.** A chart is cast automatically for the moment the user began Day 1 — their own starting gun. This is a legitimate technique with a long history: the chart of a beginning describes the beginning. Read here as *how am I meeting change? What am I actually willing to move?* Week 3 solo becomes a mirror on the user's relationship to change itself, which is the honest version of what that week is for.

**Observation without data.** The user picks a real person in their life and practises seeing them accurately for seven days — no birth data, no chart, nothing collected about them. This is closer to the original discipline than any chart is, and it's the most privacy-clean feature in the product.

Solo Week 3 runs both together. It is not a lesser version.

### The Roots cycle (later)
An optional cycle built around a parent's or grandparent's chart, with their consent where they're living. The purpose is *where did this pattern come from*, never *here is who to blame.*

Guardrail: AstroAtlas never uses a family member's chart to explain or characterise harm done to the user, and never comments on anyone's mental health. If a cycle surfaces something heavy, the product points toward real human support rather than deeper into itself.

### Then repeat
Same people, or a new circle, or solo again.

### One honesty note on "21 days"
The claim that habits form in 21 days isn't supported. <cite index="13-2">It traces to a 1960 book by the plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz about how long patients took to adjust psychologically to a new face — not habit formation.</cite> <cite index="12-1">The main study, by Phillippa Lally's team at UCL, found a median around 66 days, ranging from 18 to 254 depending on person and behaviour.</cite>

So AstroAtlas never advertises 21 days as the point where change is complete. What it can say is truer and better: 21 days is **one cycle of attention**, and repetition across cycles is where change accumulates. Maltz's original observation — adjusting to a new self-image — is actually closer to this product than habit research is.

## 4. The practice library

Every daily action comes from a library of ordinary, universal practices. **None of them require believing in astrology.** This is deliberate: the chart points attention, the practice does the work, and the work stands on its own.

| Practice | What it's for |
|---|---|
| **Breath** | Steadying yourself before looking at something uncomfortable |
| **Meditation / sitting** | Noticing a reaction without immediately obeying it |
| **Movement / yoga** | What the body is holding that the mind has explained away |
| **Journaling** | Making a pattern visible; you can't examine what stays unwritten |
| **Cooking** | Attention, sequence, and doing something concrete for someone |
| **Tidying your environment** | What you've been avoiding is usually physical too |
| **Reading** | Sustained time inside another person's interior |
| **Music** | Feeling something without needing words for it yet |
| **Changing your surroundings** | Novelty interrupting autopilot |

Two design notes:

**On travel.** Travel is a real practice but not a universal one — it costs money and time off that many users don't have, and a library billed as universal shouldn't open with a practice that isn't. The working mechanism is *novelty breaking routine perception*, which is available to everyone: a different route home, an unfamiliar part of your own city, a café you'd normally walk past. Travel becomes the largest version of that, not the entry point.

**On breathwork safety.** Gentle, slow breathing is safe for nearly everyone. Intensive techniques involving rapid over-breathing or long breath retention are not — they can cause fainting, and are contraindicated in pregnancy, epilepsy and some cardiovascular conditions. AstroAtlas ships only the gentle end of the spectrum, states plainly that it isn't medical guidance, and never instructs breath-holding in or near water.

Practices are also the natural place for user choice: the same day's chart insight can be met by whichever practice that person can actually do today.

## 5. The Galaxy that grows

You are the centre of your galaxy — not because you matter most, but because your position is the only one you can see from.

Each cycle adds a star. The Galaxy Chart is the midpoint composite of everyone currently in it: the shared field this particular set of people generates, read from where you stand.

**The galaxy brightens as you take in more of yourself** — gift *and* cost. Accepting the unflattering portrait alongside the flattering one is what makes the sky legible. Someone who sees only their strengths sees a very dim sky.

Brightness is earned through the practice, never purchasable. The moment it becomes a paid status symbol, the product has inverted its own purpose.

## 6. Method — declared openly

- **Zodiac:** tropical
- **Houses:** Placidus default, Whole Sign toggle
- **Bodies:** Sun–Pluto, Chiron, lunar nodes; asteroids off by default
- **Aspects:** Ptolemaic majors, published orb table
- **Galaxy Chart:** midpoint composite, derived Ascendant and its limits stated
- **Inception charts:** cast for the user's Day 1 moment and location
- **Later:** fixed stars and parans, kept as a separate layer rather than blended in
- **Voice:** evolutionary and choice-centred. Every delineation reads as *here is the tension* — never *here is what will happen* or *here is what you are*.

**Unknown birth time** ships from day one and matters most for older charts: houses, Ascendant and Midheaven are suppressed rather than guessed, the Moon shown as a range where it changes sign that day, and the interface says plainly what's missing. Silently defaulting someone to noon is lying to them.

## 7. How we talk about astrology

A **symbolic language for self-reflection** — a mirror, not a measurement. No claim that planetary positions cause personality or predict behaviour, and no claim of scientific validation.

The narrower claim holds up fine: a rich, non-blaming symbolic frame gives people permission to look at things they otherwise avoid, and the practices attached to it are independently worthwhile.

---

## 8. Language and wellbeing design

- No deficit language, no scores, no rankings, no compatibility percentages between people
- Never diagnostic; never names mental health conditions; never positions itself as therapy
- Forgiving streaks — a missed day doesn't reset a cycle. <cite index="13-1">Lally's data found that missing a single day didn't break habit formation</cite>; a product that punishes it is manufacturing shame for engagement
- A visible exit at every point
- Only your own chart is shareable. Galaxy views and other people's charts never are, however good they'd look on Instagram

---

## 9. Consent and privacy

- Another person's chart is added **by invitation only**; they see what will be generated and can decline
- Any participant can withdraw; their star leaves every galaxy immediately
- Weeks 1 and 2, and all journal content, stay private to their owner permanently — no export path, no team view
- Observation-only mode collects nothing about the observed person
- Deletion is real deletion
- GDPR as the baseline for all markets; mainland China data needs separate architecture and local legal review

---

## 10. Red lines

Never for hiring, promotion, pay, termination or team assignment. Never for evaluating someone by a person with power over them. No compatibility scores.

A birth date is a direct proxy for age, making any employment decision touching it a discrimination exposure. And a number invites judgement — the exact reflex this product exists to interrupt.

## 12. Technical foundations

- **Ephemeris:** Swiss Ephemeris Professional Edition, CHF 700 one-time, unlimited, valid 99 years. The free edition is AGPL and would force open-sourcing the whole platform. Self-hosted, no runtime dependency on a third party's website.
- **Atlas:** GeoNames for coordinates, IANA tzdata for historical offsets.
- **Built to last:** open formats, full user export, no lock-in.

### Regression set

| Birth data | What it tests |
|---|---|
| 16 Aug 1985, 17:25, Zhuhai CN | Baseline (founder's chart); China had no DST pre-1986 → UTC+8 |
| 16 Aug 1985, 17:40, Zhuhai CN | **Degeneracy.** 15 minutes apart — near-identical charts |
| 9 Sep 1963, 20:20, Erding DE | West Germany had no summer time in 1963 → CET, UTC+1 |
| 18 Nov 1981, 11:38, Ningbo CN | Second pre-1986 China case |

Row two is the one that matters. Two charts fifteen minutes apart are functionally the same chart. If AstroAtlas produces two visibly different readings, it's generating noise. Correct behaviour is to say so out loud — *these two are nearly identical; here is the small thing that differs.* A tool willing to admit that earns trust everywhere else.
